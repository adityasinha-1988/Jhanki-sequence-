Jhanki System Architecture with Isolated Power Domains

Everything the Gemini thread specified — ESP32, two 8-channel relay modules, 5 V 5 A supply, Blynk timing control, chain-drive trolley track — reorganised into five power domains separated by three galvanic isolation barriers, so that whatever mannequin load is plugged into whichever track station, no fault or transient has a conductive path back into the controller.

Verdict — the three rules that keep the ESP32 alive

1 · Two DC supplies, zero shared ground. ESP32 runs on its own 5 V 2 A adapter (PSU-1). Relay coils run on the 5 V 5 A SMPS (PSU-2). Pull the JD-VCC jumper on both relay boards so the optocoupler is the only thing crossing between them — no ground wire between PSU-1 and PSU-2, ever.

2 · Two AC branches after one RCCB. MCB-B feeds only the two DC supplies; MCB-A feeds only the mannequin stations and drive motor, through an E-stop + master contactor. A stalled motor trips MCB-A; the controller branch never blinks.

3 · Every inductive load gets a snubber, every channel gets a fuse. Contact-side transients are killed at the contact (RC snubber / MOV), not absorbed by the electronics. 2 A fuse per station bounds any single mannequin fault.

§1Master system diagram

One picture of the whole machine. Read left to right: mains enters once, splits into a load branch and an electronics branch; the electronics branch produces two DC rails that never touch; control intent crosses barrier B1 as light (optocoupler), becomes coil current in the dirty domain, and crosses barrier B2 as mechanical contact motion into the switched-AC domain. Track position information, if fitted, comes back only through barrier B3.

Fig 1 — Master block diagram. Purple dashed lines are the only crossings between domains: B1 is light inside an optocoupler (jumper removed ⇒ PSU-1 and PSU-2 share nothing), B2 is the relay's mechanical contact gap, B3 is an optocoupled sensor input. The switched live rail (bottom) exists only after the RCCB → MCB-A → E-stop → K1 chain, so one mushroom button de-energises every mannequin and the motor without asking the ESP32's permission.

§2Domains & isolation barriers

The partition rule is simple: a domain is a set of conductors that are allowed to share a fault. Anything you'd be sad to lose together must sit in different domains, connected only through a barrier whose isolation rating exceeds the worst transient it can see.

Why the jumper matters. With the JD-VCC jumper installed, the module ties the opto's logic side to the coil supply internally — the optocoupler is still there but isolates nothing, and every coil flyback and PSU-2 sag rides straight into whatever powers VCC. Removing it is a zero-rupee change that converts the module from "opto-decorated" to genuinely galvanically isolated. Buy boards that have the three-pin GND | VCC | JD-VCC header; boards without it cannot be split.

§3AC distribution one-line

Single-phase, one RCCB, two MCB branches. The electronics branch (MCB-B) sees only the two SMPS primaries — a near-constant ~0.1 A. Every variable, inductive, mannequin-dependent ampere lives on MCB-A, downstream of the E-stop chain.

Fig 2 — One-line diagram. The E-stop kills K1's coil, dropping the entire switched live rail — stations and motor — in one mechanical action. The ESP32 keeps running (MCB-B branch) and simply sees its outputs having no effect, which it can report on Blynk. Neutral is never switched at the channels; PE is never switched anywhere.
230 V work. Everything on this page right of the RCCB is lethal-voltage territory. Dead-work only (mains unplugged, verified with a tester), and if AC wiring isn't your daily bread, have an electrician do the D0/D3 terminations exactly to Fig 2 while you own everything left of B2.

§4Control core — the JD-VCC split & pin map

This is the single most important wiring detail in the build. Four wires from PSU-2 and sixteen-plus-one wires from the ESP32, and no ground between them.

Fig 3 — The JD-VCC split. Opto-VCC comes from the ESP32's own 3V3 so a GPIO driven HIGH puts a true 0 V across the opto LED chain — no ghost half-on channels. If a particular board's input LED chain won't strike reliably at 3V3 (a few clones need more), move opto-VCC to PSU-1's 5 V and keep everything else identical; the 1.7 V off-state differential stays safely below the two-junction threshold. Both variants preserve full galvanic isolation.

ESP32 pin map — boot-safe, 16 outputs + 2 isolated inputs

The pins Gemini's draft suggested included GPIO 2, 12 and 15 — all strapping pins. GPIO 12 high at boot reconfigures the flash voltage and can brick the boot; 2 and 15 glitch during reset, which on an active-LOW relay board means mannequins twitching every power cycle. This map avoids input-only pins (34–39 can't drive), flash pins (6–11), UART0 (1, 3) and the troublesome straps (0, 2, 12, 15):

§5Load channels & the drive motor

Your stated worry lives here: "actual mannequins connected on the track switches" means the load behind each relay is unknown at design time — this scene a 25 W geared armature motor, next scene two lamp strings, next year something else. The channel is therefore designed to a rating, not a load: 2 A fuse, snubbed contact, earthed socket. Anything within the rating can be plugged anywhere.

Fig 4 — Per-channel circuit, repeated ×15. The snubber sits across the relay contact, where the arc happens; the MOV rides with the motor it protects, so it travels with the mannequin. Without these, every contact-break on an inductive load launches a kV-scale spike that erodes contacts and radiates into signal wiring — the classic "ESP32 randomly resets when the scene changes" failure.

Drive motor circuit — CH16

The chain motor is the one load that is always inductive, always present, and mechanically coupled to people-adjacent hardware, so it gets promoted: relay CH16 switches only the small coil of contactor K2 (with an RC suppressor across the coil), and K2's main contacts carry the motor. K2's coil supply is taken from the E-stop chain, so the mushroom drops the motor even if CH16 is welded shut or the ESP32 is commanding ON. A fractional-HP geared motor (≤ 120 W, ~0.6 A run, ~4 A for the first quarter-second) sits comfortably behind an F16 = 4 A slow-blow fuse. If you later choose a bigger motor or want speed control, K2's place is taken by a single-phase speed controller — the domain structure doesn't change.

§6Track sensing — barrier B3 (optional, recommended)

The sensor-switch thread in the Gemini chat pointed the right direction: one home/index switch (reed or roller-lever microswitch) at a known point on the oval lets the firmware phase-lock the 4-minute lighting sequence to the trolley's actual position, so a scene lights up when its mannequin arrives, not on blind time. The wrong way to wire it is 3V3 logic in a long loop around the track next to AC cable — that loop is an antenna for every contact arc. The right way costs one small module:

Sensing loop12–24 V DC from a small auxiliary SMPS (or PSU-2's 5 V as a budget fallback), twisted pair around the trackVolts of noise margin instead of millivolts; twisting cancels induced pickup

§7Enclosure layout & earthing

Partition on paper only works if it's partitioned in the box. One enclosure, two zones, one controlled crossing.

Fig 5 — Physical partition. The relay boards sit astride the divider so that 230 V contact wiring never enters the DC zone and the logic ribbon never runs through the AC zone. Earthing the track steel is not optional: trolleys carry mannequins with 230 V appliances inches from a public audience — any insulation failure must find PE and trip the 30 mA RCCB, not wait for a hand.

§10Firmware guard-rails

// ---- boot-safe init: latch OFF before the pin ever drives ----
const int CH[16] = {23,22,21,19,18,17,16,4, 13,14,27,26,25,33,32,5};
for (int i = 0; i < 16; i++) {
  digitalWrite(CH[i], HIGH);      // active-LOW board: HIGH = OFF (sets output latch first)
  pinMode(CH[i], OUTPUT);         // pin wakes up already OFF — no power-on twitch

§11Bring-up checklist — first power to first show

  1. Bench, D1 only. PSU-1 + ESP32, nothing else. Flash firmware, watch serial: all 16 pins report HIGH at boot, scheduler ticks, Blynk connects.
  2. Add relay boards, no AC anywhere. Jumpers OUT, PSU-2 wired to JD-VCC/GND only, ribbon + 3V3 to VCC, pull-up packs in. Command each channel from Blynk: 16 clean clicks, correct mapping.
  3. Prove the barrier. Multimeter continuity between PSU-1 GND and PSU-2 GND: must read open. If it beeps, find the stray ground before going further — this single check is the whole point of the design.
  4. Boot-glitch audit. Power-cycle the DC side five times while watching/listening: zero relay chatter, including CH16.
  5. Dead AC build. Mains unplugged and verified dead: land RCCB → MCBs → E-stop → K1 → L-A rail → F1–16 → COM/NO → terminal rows → station cables per Fig 2/4. Torque-check every lug; tug-test every ferrule.
  6. Earth audit. Continuity < 1 Ω from the plug's PE pin to: enclosure body, each socket earth, motor frame, track steel, one trolley.
  7. First energise, no loads. Plug in, MCB-B only: PSUs up, ESP32 up. Then MCB-A: press the RCCB test button — it must trip. Reset, close E-stop chain, hear K1 pull in; hit the mushroom — K1 drops.
  8. Bulb walk. One 40–60 W lamp moved socket to socket while Blynk toggles each channel: proves mapping, fusing and polarity per station before any mannequin is trusted.
  9. Motor bump. Chain tensioned, guards on: pulse CH16 for one second, confirm direction; run one full lap, verify home pulse arrives, E-stop kill test while running.
  10. Dress rehearsal. All mannequins plugged, full 4-minute loop × 10. Feel relay and PSU temperatures (warm ok, hot not), pull the Wi-Fi router mid-show to confirm the schedule keeps running locally.
